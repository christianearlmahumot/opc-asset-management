# Fixed-Asset Management System Review of Related Literature
This review covers 25 sources in seven themes that support the proposed fixed-asset management system for the CIT-U Office of the Property Custodian (OPC) Inventory Section. Together they show that spreadsheet records and manual encoding are known sources of error, that OCR and transaction-based records can reduce them, and that few studies measure such gains in a school property office.
## 1. Spreadsheets as record systems
Spreadsheets are widely used to hold operational records, and their errors are widely accepted as common and costly. Powell et al. (2008) reviewed the research on spreadsheet errors from a manager's point of view. They concluded that although errors are accepted as common, little is known about which types of errors actually occur, and they set out research directions for reducing spreadsheet risk.
Field evidence supports that concern. Caulkins et al. (2007) interviewed 45 executives, senior managers and analysts in private, public and non-profit organizations. Of these, 44 had encountered spreadsheet errors, and most could describe errors that had led to losses or bad decisions. Error checking was mostly informal: 96% relied on a quick reasonableness check of the bottom-line figures, and only 22% used input controls. Most organizations had no formal spreadsheet quality policy.
Relevance. The OPC Inventory Section keeps its records in Excel workbooks spread across computers, with no formal checks. These studies show that this setup is a known and documented risk, which supports replacing the files with one controlled database.
## 2. Inventory record accuracy
Records that do not match physical reality are common even in organizations with mature systems. DeHoratius and Raman (2008) examined nearly 370,000 inventory records from 37 stores of one retailer and found that 65% were inaccurate. Fleisch and Tellkamp (2005) used a simulation of a retail supply chain to study how such inaccuracy affects supply chain performance, treating inventory accuracy as an operational problem rather than a clerical detail.
Relevance. These studies come from retail, not schools, but the mechanism is the same: when the record and the physical item drift apart, decisions rest on wrong numbers. In the OPC's case, items turned over as defective or transferred are deducted from one department but never added to the next. The proposed system records every movement as one transaction with a source and a destination, and the project measures the record discrepancy rate before and after.
## 3. Manual encoding and OCR document capture
Typing data by hand introduces errors, and the usual way of checking them catches few. In an experiment by Barchard and Pace (2011), visually checking typed data left 2,958% more errors than double entry, in which data are typed twice and the two versions compared. Visual checking was not significantly better than single entry with no checking, and 66% of the participants who used it produced wrong values for a reliability statistic.
Optical character recognition (OCR) offers a way to capture document text without retyping it. Smith (2007) described the architecture of Tesseract, an open-source OCR engine. Building on open-source OCR, Ha and Horák (2022) developed OCRMiner, which extracted invoice data from scanned images with 90% accuracy on English invoices and 88% on Czech invoices. Manjunath et al. (2023) built a Tesseract-based invoice extractor and tested it on more than 25 invoice types, reporting average accuracy between 85% and 95%. They also found accuracy was higher on computer-generated invoices than on scanned or photographed ones.
Relevance. The OPC re-types every sales invoice, PO and PR into the masterlist, and some documents are partly typewritten or handwritten. These studies support OCR with staff verification, set a realistic accuracy range for printed documents, and warn that accuracy drops on lower-quality images. That is why the project reports OCR accuracy separately by document type.
## 4. Asset management systems in schools
Physical assets need managing across their whole life, not only at purchase. Schuman and Brent (2005) proposed an asset life cycle management model from a case study of a large South African petrochemical company. They argued that operational reliability and systems engineering are the means to get full value from physical assets, and recommended measuring performance during the operation and support stages.
In schools and similar institutions, several systems digitize the asset record. Setiawan et al. (2019) built a web-based asset management prototype for higher education using Scrum, which stores asset data and calculates straight-line depreciation. Bandara et al. (2024) deployed an RFID system to track laboratory equipment, with a central database, cloud access and a graphical interface, and reported better accountability and resource management, though without quantitative results. Ma et al. (2021) attached QR labels to operating-theatre equipment in a hospital, each linked to an electronic manual and asset data. Search time fell from about 21 minutes to about 5 minutes for laparoscopy equipment, roughly 73% less, although the comparison was not randomized.
In the Philippines, Ayochok and Perez (2024) studied the supply and property management offices of state universities and colleges in Mountain Province. Their descriptive qualitative study found substantial inefficiency caused by manual procedures and obsolete tools, and called for digital procurement, inventory tracking and resource distribution.
Relevance. These studies confirm that schools need digital asset records and that tagging speeds up lookup. They focus on the register and on tagging, however. None of them covers the step where documents enter the record, or reconciles assets moving between departments, both of which the OPC needs.
## 5. Process automation and data quality
Repetitive clerical steps can be handed to software. Van der Aalst et al. (2018) introduced robotic process automation (RPA) as software that automates work by acting on existing systems the way a user would. Syed et al. (2020) reviewed the field and described RPA as software bots that copy user actions in existing applications, reducing the workload on staff, while noting open challenges for research and practice.
Automation only helps if the data are sound. Wang and Strong (1996) asked data consumers what quality means to them and grouped the answers into four categories: intrinsic (such as accuracy), contextual (such as completeness and timeliness), representational (such as consistent representation) and accessibility. Batini et al. (2009) compared methodologies for assessing and improving data quality and the dimensions they measure.
Relevance. The OPC builds issue orders by hand from many sheets, and its item descriptions are free text that invites duplicates and inconsistent names. The RPA studies support generating issue orders from the register. The data quality studies give the project its masterlist measures, completeness and consistency, which are checked before and after the system.
## 6. Staff turnover and knowledge loss
When experienced staff leave, organizations lose more than a person. Massingham (2018) followed an Australian public sector organization over time and found that knowledge loss showed up as lower productivity, capability gaps, lower work quality, slower task completion and longer time for new staff to become competent. It also raised risk, and the organization's knowledge base shrank and was not rebuilt by the employees who stayed.
Daghfous et al. (2013) studied knowledge loss in four manufacturing and service firms. They recommended retaining and spreading architectural knowledge, improving coordination between units, and turning capabilities into routines. They also warned that relying only on formal tools, such as standard operating procedures, information systems and databases, can weaken knowledge retention.
Relevance. At the OPC, knowledge of the Excel files leaves when staff resign, and a previous student-built system was never finished. A central system keeps each asset's history independent of any one person. Following Daghfous et al. (2013), the project pairs the system with manuals and staff training rather than relying on the software alone.
## 7. Development and evaluation methods
Development. Dingsøyr et al. (2012) reviewed a decade of research on agile methodologies and called for theory that explains how agile development works. Setiawan et al. (2019) used Scrum to build a university asset system, which shows the method fits this kind of project.
Measuring time. Lopetegui et al. (2014) reviewed 263 time-and-motion studies in healthcare. They found the term covered many different methods, from external observers to surveys, chart reviews and automatic timestamps, and proposed calling studies with an external observer recording tasks continuously "continuous observation time motion studies". That was the most common method, used in 42.2% of the studies.
Measuring usability. Bangor et al. (2008) analysed nearly ten years of System Usability Scale (SUS) data and found it a highly robust and versatile tool. They also proposed an adjective rating that matches SUS scores. Lewis (2018) reviewed the SUS's history and current use and presented the Sauro–Lewis curved grading scale, on which scores from 65.0 to 71.0 earn a C.
Acceptance and success. Davis (1989) showed that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use predict whether people accept a new information system. DeLone and McLean (2003) updated their model of information systems success, linking information quality, system quality and service quality to use, user satisfaction and net benefits.
Relevance. The project builds its MVP in Scrum sprints with demonstrations to the office. It times tasks before and after by continuous observation, and rates usability with the SUS against the Sauro–Lewis grades. Davis (1989) matters because older OPC staff already find serial-based tracking harder to adopt, so ease of use is a condition for success, not a bonus.
## 8. Synthesis and research gap
The literature agrees on the problem. Spreadsheet records are error-prone and rarely checked formally (Powell et al., 2008; Caulkins et al., 2007). Inventory records often drift from reality (DeHoratius & Raman, 2008; Fleisch & Tellkamp, 2005). Manual encoding adds errors that visual checking does not catch (Barchard & Pace, 2011). Knowledge held by staff is lost when they leave (Massingham, 2018; Daghfous et al., 2013). Philippine state universities and colleges show the same inefficiency from manual procedures (Ayochok & Perez, 2024).
The literature also offers parts of the solution, but separately:
- Asset registers and tagging in schools and hospitals (Setiawan et al., 2019; Bandara et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2021) digitize the record but do not cover document intake or the reconciliation of transfers.
- Invoice OCR reaches about 85–95% accuracy (Ha & Horák, 2022; Manjunath et al., 2023), but it has been tested on commercial invoice sets, not on a school property office's own documents.
- Evaluation: among the asset system studies reviewed, only Ma et al. (2021) measured task time before and after, and that was in a hospital, without randomization. None measured record accuracy.
Gap. Among the studies reviewed, none combines OCR-assisted document capture with transaction-based tracking of asset movements in a single school property office, and none measures the effect on time and record accuracy against a baseline. The proposed system for the OPC Inventory Section addresses that gap and tests it with continuous-observation timing, record audits, OCR field accuracy and the SUS.
